About Me:

Hi, I’m Trevor, a high school senior who has been living with erythromelalgia for roughly 5 years. I wanted to add this section to talk a bit about my experience with the condition, how I got diagnosed, what treatments I’ve tried, and what I’ve learned. I’d like to add the disclaimer that every person’s body reacts differently and many of my experiences will likely be different from others with erythromelalgia. Regardless, I hope something I say here can help anyone else going through similar experiences especially in the General Lessons sections.

*Additionally, I am not a doctor and am not trying to give medical advice, please consult your doctor before trying any treatments I describe here. This is purely a reflection on my own experiences.

Getting Diagnosed:

I was diagnosed with erythromelalgia when I was in 7th grade after I was having issues helping my family with yard work. It was minor at first where I’d feel a burning sensation in my hands and feet whenever I was out in the sun too long or in a warm room, and as time went on after first noticing, the symptoms worsened and my family thought I might have been allergic to heat. We met with my allergy doctor (I also have food allergies) to ask about cholinergic urticaria (an allergy to heat) and he said that I likely had another condition as I didn’t have hives or rashes upon exposure to heat. He suggested we look into erythromelalgia as it matched my symptoms. From there we began to research and look into an official diagnosis. Around this time (a few months after we first noticed the condition) my symptoms also seemed to stabilize in severity as fairly extreme nerve pain all over my body whenever I was hot with very quick dissipation once my temperature stabilized. After meeting with my pediatrician, we applied for a visit to Mayo Clinic as they had the only specialist in the country. At the Mayo Clinic in Minnesota, we did several tests for my nervous and endocrine systems to ensure I had proper sweating and temperature management (the sweat test unfortunately being excruciatingly painful). Luckily, we found no damage to any other parts of my body and I was officially diagnosed with Secondary Erythromelalgia with an unknown cause.

General Lessons:

There’s not much information about erythromelalgia out there, so in the last 5 years I have done a lot of my own experimenting to understand my condition and how to live with it. Once again, every body is different and my experiences may not align with your own, especially since my family thinks I may have an additional nerve condition which causes my pain to occur throughout my whole body. Regardless, I hope some of the things I have learned can help in some way!

Section 1 - What causes flairs?

What causes flairs? - From my experience, there are two primary causes: heating up and simply being hot. “Heating up” refers to going from a colder to a warmer space or warming up from exercise. Even in cold weather (40 degrees F), walking into a 65 degree room can be fairly painful and causes me to have a flare until my temperature has stabilized. For this reason, I generally avoid extreme temperatures on either end of the spectrum.

Simply being hot also causes pain, though less than changing temperatures for me. The threshold that causes pain is, in my experience, based on what my body is adjusted to, so different seasons may leave me with different tolerances. In my case, I start feeling pain above 73 F in the winter, and 79 F in the summer, just based on what I am used to. If you are overheating, I never use ice baths if I can avoid it, because most people who do often have rebound flairs and even cause further damage to their skin. Instead I 1) try to find a cooler area I can go to or 2) try to drink cold water. The best solution I have found so far is just keeping cold water (even room temperature water works pretty well, just not hot) on me at all times because it cools me down in a way that doesn't cause another flare. Finally, I try to keep the room I am in (especially my bedroom) at a stable temperature, and if I have fine control I use it to adjust myself to the changing seasons. I like to keep my room at 70 deg F during the winter and 78 during the summer, but that is very dependent on where I live. I’ll talk about some of the products I’ve found useful for this in section 3.

Interestingly, humidity really helps in my experience. I live in Colorado and my symptoms are usually worse when it's drier and, whenever I take trips to more humid areas, they are usually better. I’m not entirely sure why it helps, but it's been pretty consistent in my experience. For this reason, as well as the ability to adjust your temperature, a lot of people with erythromelalgia try to live in warm and humid areas which are not too hot.

In a similar way, exercising can be very painful as it heats me up to a certain degree. Usually after the first 20 minutes, the worst pain subsides. The main methods I’ve found that help are to slowly warm up, leading to less pain (though it takes a bit longer), or just starting with a run for a quick warm up to get the pain over with. Finally, swimming is a great option because my temperature stays really stable and cool, causing very little pain, in my experience. Even though it can be pretty rough, I really recommend continuing to stay active and exercising as research indicates that flairs don’t cause further long-term effects.

Emotion also influences temperature. Whenever I feel embarrassed or sad, it is usually very painful, so if you ever struggle with this, you’re not alone in that. Unfortunately, I haven’t found any real solutions to this other than simply doing your best to live your life in a way that keeps your happiness as a priority.

I have never gotten “used” to the pain in the sense that I felt it less, however, I do still get used to it in some sense. It’s pretty inevitable that I have flairs from time to time, and at a certain point avoiding them just isn’t worth it. For me, there’s a lot of things that I still like doing anyways, even though they cause flairs, because even if they are painful, depending on the activity, it can still be worthwhile to me. My advice to anyone with erythromelalgia is to do what you can to avoid flairs, but as you get accustomed to them, try to still live your life and do what you like in a way that works for you.

Section 2 - Treatment

My research has shown that it depends on the person, however, there are a lot of treatments for symptoms that may or may not work. The ones I’ve heard of include some chronic pain medications (learn more about them in the mayo clinic report), hot water soaks, and many new treatments for nerve damage and pain, such as sodium channel blockers. From my limited knowledge, the more mild medications are a good starting point due to the minimal side effects. Next, the hot water soaks show quite a bit of promise, however, you should thoroughly research it first because there isn’t much official research yet (I included a helpful facebook group focused on it in the resources section of the EM page called “Erythromelalgia, Fight Fire With Fire!”). Finally, stronger pain medications can be worth it, depending on how much you struggle with the pain vs how you experience the side effects.

I’ve heard of each of these treatments improving symptoms as well as having no noticeable effect depending on the person. Because of the various potential side effects each treatment could involve, different ones are worth trying for different people. In that regard, it's really important to research the treatments you are interested in and weigh the potential side effects against how difficult your symptoms are for you. Outside of what I described in section 1, I personally have tried a topical amitriptyline, ketamine cream, and hot water soaks. Here’s my experiences with each of them:

The topical cream was prescribed to me by the doctor I met at Mayo Clinic and I applied it on my feet and the backs of my hands for about 6 months. Unfortunately, my symptoms saw no improvement, though this definitely shouldn't stop others from trying. I don't have too much to comment on this one, aside from that mine was a standard topical cream so it was a little inconvenient when applied, but I could still work on small tasks like homework.

My mom and I learned about the hot water soaks from an online erythromelalgia group that saw a lot of success by using them. To do it, we bought water heating foot baths where I’d soak my feet in them for 30 minutes each night while I worked at my desk (from what I know, most forms of hot water exposure can work). We would set the heat to 95 F at the start and 105 F towards the later months. I would soak my feet under my desk for the allotted time. Putting your feet in the water is initially painful, though after the first 5 minutes, the pain subsides a bit and it’s pretty doable. We kept up with this for roughly 1.5 years and saw a big improvement in my symptoms. I had a higher tolerance to heat and the pain I felt was generally less. The main drawback was that the pain each day was really exhausting, so after I started getting busy, it was too difficult to balance the extra sleep with school and extracurriculars and I decided it was fine as it was. While the hot water treatment didn’t cure my symptoms, it did ease them a bit when I was still figuring out how to live with my condition, so I am happy we stuck with it for as long as we did.

Section 3 - Things That Help

The first thing my family got after my diagnosis was a personal air conditioner for my room that hangs on my window and has been a big help. With it, I can cool my room however I need and it's great for matching my room temperature to the current season. These can vary significantly in convenience, noisiness, price, and effectiveness, though for me, getting one was definitely worthwhile.

The other thing that has really made a difference for me is water-cooled bedding. It can be a bit pricey, but sleeping can be really difficult with the condition due to waking up from overheating and having a hard time falling asleep for the same reason. I specifically have the 8Sleep Pod 3 and it has helped me a lot. We had some issues with it at first when we ran into an issue; the customer service was pretty difficult to get through, but the right person eventually helped us, and after it was fixed, it has been very worthwhile. I also tried the SleepMe Chilipad and it helped with temperature, though not as effectively or comfortably.
